“How often do I actually have to post?” is one of the questions we are asked most often. Online you will find plenty of very definite answers – daily, three times a week, five times a week – that contradict one another.

The honest answer is less catchy, but more useful: there is no number that applies to everyone. There is, however, a sensible starting point and a simple way of finding your own rhythm.

## Why there is no single right number

How Instagram distributes content changes constantly, and nobody on the outside knows all the details. Anyone who sells you an exact ideal frequency as fact is usually generalising from their own experience. On top of that, businesses are very different:

- A restaurant with a lunch menu that changes daily has a genuine reason to post every day.
- A hair salon has a steady stream of before-and-after results, but little time between appointments.
- A specialist shop may have new products less often, but a great deal of expert advice to share.

What does show up in practice time and again, though: profiles that regularly publish content their target audience can do something with develop better than those that post in bursts and then fall silent for weeks.

## A rule of thumb as a starting point

So that you don’t start from scratch, here is a guide that has proved its worth as a way in for local businesses. It is a starting value for testing, not a promise.

| Level | Posts and Reels | Stories | A good fit if … |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Minimum | 1 a week | on 1–2 days | you are on your own and want to build up consistency first |
| Solid | 2–3 a week | on 3–5 days | you set aside fixed time or someone in the team helps |
| Ambitious | 4 or more a week | almost daily | you have reasons to post every day or have handed over management |

Most small businesses do well with the middle level. If you are only just starting out or have posted irregularly so far, feel free to start with the minimum and keep it up for eight weeks. That is worth more than an ambitious plan that is abandoned after three weeks.

## Consistency beats quantity

Imagine two businesses. The first publishes fifteen posts in January and then nothing more until March. The second posts every week on Tuesdays and Fridays. Overall, both have published a similar amount – but only the second is reliably present when someone is wondering where to eat at the weekend or who should do their next haircut.

Consistency has another practical effect: you get better. If you produce every week, you find routines, recognise what goes down well and, over time, need considerably less effort per post.

<Callout title="The stress test for your plan">
Take your busiest week of the year as the benchmark: the Christmas trade, the start of the university term, the summer terrace. The rhythm you can still manage even then is your realistic baseline. Anything beyond that is a bonus for quieter times.
</Callout>

## How to find your own rhythm

Instead of relying on a general recommendation, you can use a simple test to find out for yourself what works for you.

1. **Note your starting position.** From the insights for the last four weeks, write down: accounts reached, profile visits, link clicks, messages.
2. **Set the frequency.** Choose a level from the table and fix the days of the week.
3. **Keep it up for four weeks.** During this time, change as little else as possible, otherwise you won’t know in the end what caused any change.
4. **Compare.** Look at the same figures again. Pay less attention to likes than to what leads to your goal: profile visits, link clicks, enquiries.
5. **Adjust.** If something has improved and the effort was manageable, stick with it or increase slightly. If it was too much, go back a level. Then test again.

Four weeks is a lower limit here. Individual posts fluctuate widely, and seasonal effects – school holidays, public holidays, the weather – can distort the picture. We look in more depth at which key figures are really meaningful in the article [Measuring social media success](post:measuring-social-media-success).

## Which format, how often?

Not every format needs the same pace.

- **Reels** take more effort, but are often also shown to people who don’t follow you yet. Even one a week can be a good start for a small business. Our [Reels guide](post:reels-guide-local-business) explains how to produce them efficiently.
- **Photo posts and carousels** are suitable for offers, new products, tips and before-and-after results. They are quicker to create and fill the gaps between Reels.
- **Stories** are the format for everyday life: the offer of the day, free appointments, a look behind the scenes. They are allowed to be imperfect and take hardly any time.

A beauty salon, for example, could plan like this: a care tip as a carousel on Tuesdays, a short Reel from a treatment on Fridays, plus Stories whenever an appointment becomes free at short notice.

## How to keep up the rhythm in everyday business

A fixed rhythm rarely fails for lack of ideas; it fails because of organisation. These habits help:

- **Batching:** Produce several posts in one go at a fixed time instead of starting afresh every day.
- **Introducing series:** A recurring format such as “Dish of the week” or “Question of the week” saves you the search for ideas.
- **Scheduling ahead:** Schedule posts in advance so that the channel keeps running even when things are busy in the business.
- **Building up a reserve:** Keep two or three timeless posts in reserve – for illness, holidays or days when nothing works.
- **Collecting as you go:** Take short clips and photos during the day whenever something looks good. That way you have material when your production slot comes round.

Anyone who posts without a plan quickly ends up in one of the typical patterns we describe in [common social media mistakes](post:common-social-media-mistakes).

## How to tell that you are posting too much or too little

**Signs of too much:** You publish posts you don’t stand behind yourself, just to fulfil the plan. The content repeats itself. You no longer reply to messages and comments because production eats up all your time. Or the reactions per post fall noticeably over several weeks.

**Signs of too little:** Regular customers know nothing about your new offer. Your last post is so old that visitors wonder whether you are still in business. You actually have plenty to show, but never get round to it.

In the second case the cause is almost always a lack of time, not a lack of will. Then it can make sense to hand over planning and implementation – for example through ongoing [social media management](page:service:social-media-management), where the rhythm no longer depends on your day-to-day business.

The bottom line: start with a frequency you can definitely manage, watch your own figures and adjust in small steps. Your best rhythm is the one you are still keeping to in six months’ time.
