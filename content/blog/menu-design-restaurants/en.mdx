The menu is the one document every guest picks up – and yet it often comes about on the side: grown over the years, with prices stuck over and dishes nobody has ordered for a long time. And yet it helps decide how quickly guests find their way around and what impression your business makes.

This guide goes through the points you should settle for a new or reworked menu: from the structure, typeface and material to the file for the printer.

## Structure and reading flow

Arrange the menu in the order in which guests order. The usual sequence is: drinks or aperitif, starters, main courses, side dishes, desserts.

- **Clear sections:** Every group gets a clearly visible heading. Guests look for the section first, then the dish.
- **Consistent entries:** Name, short description, price – always in the same order and in the same place.
- **Short descriptions:** Name the most important ingredients and how the dish is prepared. One or two lines are usually enough.
- **Highlights used sparingly:** If you mark house specialities, mark only a few. If everything is highlighted, nothing stands out any more.
- **Labels with a key:** Symbols for vegetarian, vegan or spicy only help if they are explained.

## How many dishes?

A long menu suggests a wide choice, but it also means more stock, more preparation and longer decisions at the table. A shorter menu is easier to cook at consistent quality and easier to lay out clearly.

In practice, that means: go through your menu dish by dish. What is ordered regularly, what fits your concept, what just ties up storage space? Variations of the same dish can often be combined in one entry instead of listing each one separately.

## Typography and legibility

Guests read your menu by candlelight, on the terrace in the sun or without their reading glasses. Design it for those situations, not for the screen in the office.

- **Type size:** better a little larger. Print out a test page and read it at the table in the lighting you actually have in the evening.
- **Contrast:** dark type on a light background is the safest option. Light type on dark paper or text over photos quickly becomes hard to read.
- **Few typefaces:** one for headings and one for the text is enough. Use ornate typefaces only for short headings.
- **Spacing:** enough air between the entries so that the eye stays on the line.
- **Prices:** in the same typeface and size as the entry, written consistently and clearly assigned to the dish.

## Photos: yes or no?

Photos help where guests don’t know a dish or need to decide quickly – in a snack bar, for example, with international cuisine or on a delivery menu. In a restaurant with a calm, upmarket look, a purely typographic menu often fits better.

If you do use photos, use only a few, photographed consistently and showing your own dishes. The picture must show what actually ends up on the plate. You should do without stock photos: they raise expectations your dish cannot meet.

## Formats and materials

Which format fits depends on how often what you offer changes and how much wear the menu gets.

| Option | Suitable for | Bear in mind |
|---|---|---|
| Single sheet, printed on both sides | small menus that change often | wears out quickly, but is inexpensive to reprint |
| Folded menu | medium-sized menus with a fixed selection | changes mean a reprint |
| Folder or clipboard with loose sheets | menus with changing parts | individual pages can be swapped |
| Bound menu | extensive menus that rarely change | high quality, but inflexible |

As for the material: menus are handled, splashed and wiped down. Heavier paper, a protective coating or a wipeable surface extend their life. Ask your printer for samples and hold them in your hand before you decide.

## Digital menu, PDF and QR code

A digital menu can be changed in minutes and incurs no printing costs. In return, it depends on reception, battery and guests’ willingness to reach for their phone at the table.

- **Advantages:** quick changes to prices and dishes, no reprint, and it can also be linked on your website and profiles.
- **Disadvantages:** not every guest gets on with it, a multi-page PDF in print format is tedious to read on a phone, and you yourself are often the last to notice an outdated link.

If you use a QR code menu, design it for a phone screen: single column, large type, small file. Test the code every time you replace the file – and always keep printed copies available.

Use a fixed address for your digital menu and only replace the file behind it. That way the QR codes on the tables, the link on your website and the reference in your profiles remain valid when the menu changes.
</Callout>

## Takeaway, delivery, seasons and languages

### Takeaway and delivery menus

A takeaway menu has a different job from the menu at the table. It needs a phone number, how to order, opening and delivery times, the delivery area and, where applicable, a minimum order value – clearly visible, not in the small print. List only dishes that travel well.

### Seasonal inserts

Asparagus, game, a Christmas menu: offers like these belong on a separate sheet that matches the design of the main menu. That way the main menu stays valid for longer, and you can respond at short notice.

### Multilingual menus

If you have many international guests, a second language is worthwhile. Either the translation appears in smaller type beneath each entry, or you keep a separate menu for each language. Have translations checked by someone who really has a command of the language – and carry every price change through to all versions.

## Keeping prices and mandatory information up to date

A menu with prices stuck over or corrected by hand looks careless, and differing prices between the menu, the till and the website lead to arguments. So decide who updates every place when something changes: the printed menu, the notice on display, the till system, the website, the digital menu and delivery platforms.

In Germany there are also statutory requirements on what information guests must be given – including on allergens, additives and price information. The requirements can change and depend on your business. Clarify the current position with your IHK (Chamber of Industry and Commerce), DEHOGA (the German hotel and restaurant association) or your adviser before you print. ## Print files: what the printer needs

Most complaints arise not from the design but from the file. These are the terms you should know:

- **Bleed:** Backgrounds and images that run to the edge must extend a few millimetres beyond the final format. Otherwise white edges appear when the sheets are trimmed.
- **Safety margin:** Text and prices do not belong right at the edge, because the trim can vary slightly.
- **Colour mode:** Screens work with RGB, printing presses generally with CMYK. Bright screen colours often come out more muted in print.
- **Resolution:** Images from the internet or from messaging apps are usually too small for printing. Around 300 dpi at the printed size is usual.
- **Fonts:** embedded or converted to outlines, so that nothing gets substituted.
- **File format:** generally a print-ready PDF.

Your printer’s specifications are always what counts, because bleed, colour profile and format differ from one provider to the next.

## Checklist before printing

1. Do all the prices match the till and the website?
2. Are the dishes, descriptions and sections complete and up to date?
3. Has the mandatory information been checked and agreed with your adviser or your association?
4. Has a second person proofread the spelling, figures and translations?
5. Is the menu easy to read in the lighting of your dining room?
6. Are the address, phone number, opening hours and web address correct?
7. Does the QR code work, and does it lead to the current file?
8. Does the file meet the printer’s specifications – format, bleed, colour mode, resolution?
