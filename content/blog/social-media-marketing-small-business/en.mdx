You run a café, a salon, a studio or a shop and know that you “really ought to be doing more on social media”. Usually it isn’t the will that is lacking but the time – and an answer to the question of where to start at all.

This guide shows you an approach that suits a small business: one goal, one channel, a few fixed formats and a rhythm you can keep up even in busy weeks. Without chasing every trend and without having to be in front of the camera every day.

## What social media can do for your business – and what it can’t

Many people look at a profile before they try a new restaurant or book an appointment. Your presence is then something like a shop window that stays open after closing time. Realistically, social media can do four things above all for a local business:

- **Staying visible:** People who already know you are regularly reminded of you.
- **Building trust:** People see what your place looks like, who works there and how the result turns out.
- **Creating occasions:** The new lunch dish, free appointments on Thursday, the course starting in January.
- **Making contact easier:** For many people, a short message is easier than a phone call.

What social media can’t do: rescue a weak offering or fill the shop overnight. Nobody can credibly promise you a particular reach or a particular level of revenue. But you certainly can influence how clearly, regularly and likeably you present yourself.

## Step 1: Set a specific goal

“More reach” is not a goal you can work with. Ask yourself instead: what should someone do after seeing your post?

- **Restaurant or café:** reserve a table, drop by at lunchtime
- **Hair or beauty salon:** book an appointment online
- **Gym:** enquire about a trial session
- **Retail:** come into the shop or have a product put aside

Choose one main goal. Almost everything else follows from it: which link belongs in your profile, which call to action goes under your posts and which number you keep an eye on later.

## Step 2: Choose the right channel

You don’t have to be everywhere. Two questions are decisive: where are your customers? And what kind of content can you really deliver in everyday life?

| Channel | Strength for local businesses | Effort |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Instagram | Images, Reels and Stories; good for anything you can see – food, hairstyles, premises, products | medium, thrives on consistency |
| Facebook | Events, local groups, often a somewhat older base of regular customers | low, if you reuse Instagram content |
| TikTok | short, entertaining videos; often a younger audience | higher, because almost only video counts |

For many local businesses, Instagram is in practice a sensible starting point, because photos, short videos and quick everyday glimpses can be combined there. If you are wavering between two platforms, our comparison [Instagram or TikTok](post:instagram-vs-tiktok) will help.

## Step 3: Set up your profile properly

Before you plan the first post, your profile should answer the most important questions in a few seconds. Go through this list once:

1. **Name and username:** the way people search for you – ideally with location or industry if your name alone says little.
2. **Profile picture:** your logo or a clear photo that is recognisable even when small.
3. **Bio:** What do you offer, for whom and where? One sentence is enough.
4. **Link:** straight to the goal from step 1, i.e. to reservations, appointment booking or directions.
5. **Contact:** keep address, phone number and opening hours up to date.
6. **Highlights:** fixed answers to recurring questions, such as prices, menu, team or directions.

Bear in mind that business profiles in Germany may be required to provide a Legal Notice (Impressum). If in doubt, have this checked once rather than ignoring it.

## Step 4: Find topics you can return to again and again

The most common hurdle is not the technology but the question: “What should I post today?” The answer is fixed topic areas, often called content pillars. Three to four are enough:

- **Offering:** What can people get from you? Dishes, treatments, courses, new products.
- **People:** Who is behind it? The team, skilled hands at work, a look into the kitchen or workshop.
- **Knowledge:** What do customers constantly ask you? Every question is a post.
- **News:** free appointments, changed opening hours, promotions, events.

<Callout title="Example: a café in Münster">
On Mondays the weekly special as a photo, on Wednesdays a short Reel from the bakery, on Fridays a look at the cake counter for the weekend – plus Stories whenever something comes fresh out of the oven. Three fixed formats that repeat every week and still always look new.
</Callout>

Recurring formats have two advantages: you don’t have to think afresh every time, and your followers know what to expect from you.

## Step 5: Set a rhythm and evaluate

It is better to plan small and reliable. As a rule of thumb for getting started, two to three posts a week plus Stories on several days have proved their worth. That is a starting point, not a law. You can read more about finding your own pace in the article [How often should you post on Instagram?](post:instagram-posting-frequency).

This is how the effort stays manageable:

- **Batching:** Set aside a fixed hour once a week or every two weeks and produce several posts in one go.
- **Scheduling ahead:** Posts can be scheduled in advance. Then the channel keeps running even when the shop is full.
- **Responding:** Reply to comments and messages promptly. That is often the most direct route to a booking.

Look at your insights once a month. Three questions are enough to begin with: which posts were saved or shared most often? How many people visited your profile or tapped the link? And are enquiries coming in that refer to your content? Do more of what works and drop formats that repeatedly interest nobody. We show you how to read key figures sensibly in the article [Measuring social media success](post:measuring-social-media-success).

## Do it yourself or hand it over?

Either can be right. Doing it yourself fits if you enjoy it and reliably block out time for it. Handing it over is worthwhile if the channel keeps going quiet, if you lack ideas or images, or if your time is simply better invested in the business.

A simple check: have you posted regularly over the last eight weeks? If not, it is rarely down to a lack of motivation, but to a lack of capacity. In that case, ongoing [social media management](page:service:social-media-management) can make sense, with planning, content and community care all coming from a single source. You still remain the most important source – nobody knows your business and your customers as well as you do.

Whether you take it on yourself or get support: start with the smallest plan you can definitely keep up. You can always expand later.
